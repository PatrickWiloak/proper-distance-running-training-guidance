# Running in Heat, Humidity and Altitude: Pace Adjustment and Acclimatization

Heat, humidity and altitude all make the same effort slower. This guide covers how much to slow down, how to adapt over time, and what the evidence does and does not support.

## The Short Answer

**Run the effort, not the number.** Every pace in this guide, including the [VDOT and Pace Tables](../reference/vdot-and-pace-tables.md), assumes cool weather, flat ground and sea level. When conditions are worse, the same pace costs more, so the same effort produces a slower pace. Slowing down on a hot day is not fitness loss. It is the correct response.

In practice:

- **Easy runs:** drop the pace target entirely. Run by feel (conversational, RPE 3-4) and let the watch say whatever it says. See [Heart Rate Zones](../concepts/heart-rate-zones.md) for why heart rate is also unreliable in heat.
- **Workouts:** keep the *effort* and the *recovery structure*, and accept slower splits. A threshold session in 28°C (82°F) run at the same effort will produce slower splits than the table, and that is the intended stimulus. Forcing the table pace in the heat turns a threshold session into a much harder one.
- **Races:** adjust the goal before the start. Heat degrades marathon results measurably, and the penalty is larger for slower runners because they are exposed for longer.[1] See [Race Day Execution](../concepts/race-day-execution.md).
- **Altitude:** expect a slower pace at the same effort from the first day, and expect it to improve only partly.
- **Safety first:** heat illness is a medical emergency and gets worse rapidly if you keep going. The warning signs are listed in [Race Day Execution](../concepts/race-day-execution.md) and apply to training runs too.

## Why Heat Slows You Down

Running makes heat. Most of the energy you burn is released as heat rather than forward motion, and your body has to move that heat to the skin and into the air. It does this by diverting blood to the skin and by sweating.

That creates a competition for blood. The muscles want oxygen, the skin wants to shed heat, and the heart can only pump so much. In heat, heart rate climbs at a given pace (often called cardiovascular drift), stroke volume falls as you lose fluid, and core temperature rises. Perceived effort rises with it, and you slow down, partly because the brain reduces how hard you are willing to push as core temperature rises. Whether that slowing is a protective brain-level decision or a purely physical limit is still debated in the research literature, but the practical result is the same.

**Humidity matters because sweat only cools you when it evaporates.** In humid air, sweat drips off instead. You lose the fluid without getting the cooling. This is why 24°C (75°F) at high humidity can feel worse than 32°C (90°F) in dry air. Direct sun, low wind and hot pavement add load on top of the air temperature.

## How Much Slower?

There is no single exact formula, and anyone who offers one with false precision is overselling. What the evidence supports:

- **Performance falls as temperature rises above a cool optimum.** Population data on marathon finishers show the fastest times in cool conditions and progressively slower times as it warms; see [Race Day Execution](../concepts/race-day-execution.md) for the temperature at which the penalty starts to matter.[1] The study also found that slower finishers lose more time than faster ones in the same conditions.
- **Humidity and sun add to the penalty.** Researchers often use wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT), which combines air temperature, humidity and radiant heat from the sun (and, depending on the version, wind), rather than air temperature alone. WBGT values read lower than air temperature, so do not compare them with air-temperature figures directly.

### A Rule of Thumb: Temperature Plus Dew Point

A popular running heuristic adds the air temperature and the dew point (both in °F) and slows pace as the sum rises. It is widely shared among coaches, but it is a coaching rule of thumb, not a result from a controlled study, and the exact slowdown percentages vary by source. Treat it as a way to notice that humidity counts, not as a calculator.

| Temperature + dew point (°F) | Rough meaning |
|---|---|
| Under about 100 | Little or no adjustment needed |
| About 100-120 | Expect a small slowdown |
| About 120-140 | Expect a clear slowdown; shorten or soften quality sessions |
| Over about 140 | Large slowdown; consider moving the session indoors or to the cooler part of the day |

Those bands are approximate by design. What matters is the direction: the dew point tells you how well sweat will work.

### What to Actually Do With the Number

1. **Check the dew point, not just the temperature.** Most weather apps show it.
2. **Run early or late.** Moving a run from 2 p.m. to 6 a.m. is worth more than any adaptation you can train.
3. **Convert workouts to effort targets.** For a threshold run, aim for "comfortably hard" (RPE about 7) rather than a split. For intervals, hold the effort and extend the recovery if you need to.
4. **Cut volume of hard work before you cut easy volume.** If a quality session is turning into a survival run, stop it early. A truncated good session beats a finished bad one.
5. **Do not trust a goal-pace plan for a hot race.** Set the pace in the first miles by effort and err slow. Time lost to starting too fast in the heat is rarely recovered later.

## Heat Acclimatization

The body adapts to heat quickly compared with most training adaptations, and the adaptations are well documented.

**What changes:**

- Blood plasma volume expands, which supports stroke volume and lowers heart rate at a given pace.
- Sweating starts earlier and the sweat rate rises.
- Sweat becomes more dilute, so less salt is lost per litre.
- Core temperature and heart rate during a standard run are lower, and the same effort feels easier.

**How long it takes:** most of the benefit arrives within roughly 1-2 weeks of regular exposure, with additional smaller gains after that. A common protocol in the research is about 60-90 minutes a day of exercise in the heat for 10-14 days, but many recreational runners get a good share of the benefit from the ordinary approach: keep running in the heat at easy effort, build gradually, and do not stack hard sessions on the hottest days.

**How to do it safely:**

- Start with easy runs and shorter durations in the heat. Add duration before intensity.
- Drink to thirst during runs and replace losses afterward. Do not force large volumes of plain water; the [Fueling and Nutrition](./fueling-and-nutrition.md) page covers hydration and its risks in both directions.
- Heat adds recovery cost. A hot easy run stresses the body more than a cool one, so treat a heat block as extra load and respect [Recovery and Adaptation](./recovery.md).

**How long it lasts:** heat adaptation fades over a period of weeks without exposure. It is also partly specific to the kind of heat. If your goal race is hot, practising in similar heat in the final few weeks matters more than having been acclimatized in spring.

**Realistic expectations:** acclimatization narrows the gap but does not close it. You will still be slower in the heat than in the cold, only less so than before you adapted.

### Heat Training for a Cool Race

Some athletes use deliberate heat exposure (hot baths or sauna after runs, or training in the heat) hoping to improve performance in cool conditions. Some studies suggest a benefit and others do not. The evidence is mixed and the effect, if real, is modest, so this is optional and not a substitute for solid training.

## Altitude

### What Altitude Does

At altitude the air is not "thinner" in oxygen percentage; the pressure is lower, so each breath delivers less oxygen into the blood. Maximal oxygen uptake falls, and with it the pace you can hold at a given effort. See [VO2 Max and Running Economy](../concepts/vo2max-economy.md) for what VO2 max is and why it matters.

How much it drops depends on elevation and the individual. Research in endurance athletes found VO2 max and performance falling roughly in proportion to elevation, with sizeable differences between individuals.[2] The higher you go, the larger the loss.

**Practical consequences:**

- Race pace is slower at altitude and the slower pace is not a fitness measure.
- Efforts that are aerobic at sea level feel harder: breathing rate rises immediately.
- Short, fast efforts (R pace, 200-400 m repeats) are affected less than sustained aerobic efforts, because they depend less on oxygen delivery. Long threshold and marathon efforts are affected more.
- Easy running should be run by effort. Expect the pace to be slower, and do not chase sea-level numbers.

### Acclimatization to Altitude

Your body responds to altitude by breathing harder immediately and, over days to weeks, by increasing red blood cell production and other adjustments. The practical picture:

- The first days are the hardest. Sleep may be disturbed and heart rate is elevated. Keep early runs easy and short.
- Improvement continues over roughly two to three weeks, though it does not bring performance back to sea-level values.
- Hard efforts early in the stay carry more risk of overreaching. Give the body time before doing key workouts.

### Racing at Altitude: Timing

If you travel to race at altitude, two common approaches exist:

- **Arrive as close to the race as possible** (within the day or two before). Acclimatization has not started, but neither have the worst of the early symptoms. This is the usual choice when there is no time to stay.
- **Stay long enough to acclimatize** (about two weeks or more). Better in principle, but costs time and routine, and the disruption can offset the gain.

The middle ground, arriving several days ahead, is generally considered the worst option because symptoms and sleep disruption peak around then. Individual response varies widely, so past experience at altitude is the best guide.

### Altitude Training to Improve Sea-Level Racing

Elite runners spend time at altitude hoping to improve sea-level performance. The evidence supports a more careful claim than the popular belief:

- The best-supported model is "live high, train low": sleeping at moderate altitude while doing quality sessions at lower elevation, which was tested in a well-known study of collegiate runners.[3]
- Results vary substantially between individuals, and some athletes do not respond.
- Merely living or training at altitude without the ability to run hard sessions may reduce training quality.
- Altitude camps cost time, money and recovery, and are not a requirement for improvement. Most runners will gain far more from consistent, well-structured training at home.

## Putting It Together: Common Situations

**Summer training in a hot city.** Run early, drop pace targets on easy days, convert hard sessions to effort targets, and expect pace to improve through the season as you acclimatize. Fall racing often feels like a gift for this reason.

**Racing a hot marathon.** Set a goal that accounts for the forecast, start slower than the cool-weather plan would suggest, take fluids at aid stations, and be willing to back off further if symptoms appear.

**Traveling to altitude for a race.** Pick a strategy (arrive late or stay long), run the first sessions by effort, and treat the finishing time as a conditions-adjusted result.

**Using a treadmill.** Indoors is a good heat refuge, but with poor airflow, indoor running can be warmer than it seems. Use a fan and let effort, not the belt speed, be your guide.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Chasing table paces in the heat.** The result is a harder workout than the plan intended, higher injury and illness risk, and a bad recovery.
2. **Reading a slow hot run as lost fitness.** It is the conditions.
3. **Ignoring humidity.** The dew point often matters as much as the temperature.
4. **Over-drinking.** Fluid needs rise in the heat, but so does the risk of overdrinking; drink to thirst and see [Fueling and Nutrition](./fueling-and-nutrition.md).
5. **Starting a hot race at cool-weather pace.** The cost arrives in the last third.
6. **Hard sessions in the first days at altitude.** Give the body time.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Pace tables assume cool weather and sea level.** Outside those conditions, hold the effort and accept the slower number.
2. **Dew point counts as much as air temperature.** Sweat only cools when it evaporates.
3. **Heat acclimatization is real and fast.** Roughly one to two weeks of regular exposure gives most of the benefit, and it fades without exposure.
4. **Altitude cuts aerobic performance from the first day.** Acclimatization helps but does not fully restore it.
5. **Slower runners lose more in heat** because they are exposed longer.
6. **Evidence for altitude and heat "training camps" improving cool, sea-level racing is mixed.** Do not buy the idea that it is required.
7. **Heat illness is an emergency.** Stop, cool down and get help.
